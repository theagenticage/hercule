The InboxHi-fi design, session B Open the window

Lasting work wide, small requests narrow.

This is where every outside thing that concerns you lands, before you take it on. Your tickets lie wide, as rows on the raised surface of the project they are part of, with their status first. Review requests and mail lie in a narrow column of small, quieter cards, built to be dealt with fast. The shape of a card says how much attention it deserves, and nothing needs a label.

What changed, and why.

The last round was a deck: stage columns, big numbers, piles that hid unrelated things. The founder found the columns arbitrary, the numbers dashboardy, the stacks unexplained, and the layout too uniform. He liked sketch A because it was less monotone: a small column with one card structure beside a main area with another.

  • No stages. To review, To answer and To finish are gone. Things are ordered simply.
  • No hero numbers, no brass rule. The display face is the logo's alone. Counts are few, small and quiet, and a section title is plain words.
  • Nothing folds. Every card is visible, and the page scrolls. If a count had to say how much is waiting, the work has already grown too big.
  • Pull requests are small. They are transient, and they ask little. One linked to a ticket lives on that ticket. The only ones that stand alone are colleagues' review requests, and those go in the narrow column.

Lasting work shows the bigger picture.

A ticket lives for weeks, so where it sits matters. A project is one surface: its name, how it stands, how far along the source says it is, and what it is part of. Each ticket is a row with its status first, because here status is the point, then its title, its milestone, and the last thing that happened.

Part of is a graph, not a ladder. A project can sit in two initiatives, and a team is another path, not a rung. Nothing is ever shown as if it had one parent. A ticket in two projects shows under each, once as itself and once as a plain pointer, and it is counted once. Group by offers whatever levels the kinds declare.

Progress appears only where the source reports it. The cards here are only what concerns you, so any sum from them would be wrong. Browser test reliability reports none, and shows none.

A stack needs a reason you can see.

There is one good reason left to put things together: they are directly linked. A ticket with its pull request. That pull request also asks you for a review. Under the ticket's title, each linked thing is a row, and all of them are always there.

  • Which leads. The lasting thing. The pull request rides on its ticket, not the other way round.
  • Where it sits. With its lead, under its project. The urgent part says so on its own row, with what it asks of you first, then its wait, and its moves on hover. It never drags the ticket out of its place.
  • How a signal shows. It lands on the row. The row lights and changes its words, and nothing moves.
  • Top and stack are different. A click on the ticket opens the ticket. A click on a row opens that row.

Every link says who claims it, quietly, on hover. The source reports it, or a person made it. A reference found in text is weaker: it stays a hint, Mentioned in #6263, and joins nothing until someone confirms it. Sharing a repository or a project never joins anything. A link can point at something you cannot open, and its row says so.

A person can link two things by hand: confirm a hint, search in the card details, or carry one card onto another.

Requests are built to be dealt with fast.

A title and one line. The column's title already says these are requests, so the usual one needs only a name: Juno · storefront-web. Only what is unusual gets words: Felix asked again, Checks failed. Hover, and the moves are there by name. The close sits at the same spot on every card, so the same click at the same spot works down the column.

Signals live inside their entity.

The card details keep a timeline of everything that happened to a thing. Links make it richer: a ticket's timeline weaves in what happened to its pull request and its error, each with its logo, so the whole story reads in one place.

A new signal gets the one flourish the Inbox allows itself: a drop falls on the line, the words roll over, and a rim of light fades in the colour of the news. Then everything is still again.

No view knows a system.

Every card below comes from one renderer. A kind fills in a table: its noun, its mark, which card shape it deserves, how patient it is, its statuses, what it is usually grouped under. A plugin reports its relations as data. Quayside is a plugin I made up. It has its own hierarchy, a delivery round in a site, and it needed a table and no code. A test fails if any view names a system.

The fixed set of status shapes

Handing off should feel like giving it to a capable colleague.

Hover a row or a request and its moves appear by name: the workflow that fits, Take it, a close. With one workflow it is one click. With several, a small menu names them. Press H, or pick the card up and drop it on a colleague.

Then it plays as one chain, and the cause always comes first. The colleague rises into place at the end of the line, the sentence rolls to Agent review has it, and the card's own mark lifts off and flies to Overview, whose icon dips to receive it. Undo plays the whole thing backwards.

One cursor, and a rhythm.

The pointer and the keyboard share one current card, and every move moves on to the next that waits. Arrows follow the page by where things lie. J and K read through it, and they walk inside an open sheet too. Press ? in the window for the keys.

Dismiss works like closing a browser tab: the next card rises into the place, so the same click at the same spot dismisses the next. Undo goes as far back as you like, from anywhere.

Intake stays the noisy layer. Things rise out of it.

Intake moves behind the Inbox, the way Office sits behind Overview: one pill, Inbox with Cards and Intake, and one sidebar row fewer. It is grouped by entity, so an error with 200 events is one line. This is a sketch, as asked.

Every state is designed.

The Inbox at zero is a small sunrise: Hercule's head comes up over a horizon, and the rays draw themselves out from it. When the requests alone run out, a smaller one rises in the column. First run is the real page: each side asks for its sources. Loading uses the real cards with their words held back, so nothing shifts when they arrive. Nothing fails silently: offline and a failing source each say what they mean for what you see.

What I rejected.

DESIGN.md keeps the full list. The deck of piles hid things behind an unrelated top card. Stage columns sorted by a label nobody needs. Grouping everything connected would pull in the neighbourhood, so only direct, confirmed links join. And my first reading of sketch A kept pull requests in the big area, which was backwards: what the founder liked was the two shapes.
