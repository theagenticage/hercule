Seeing all the work

Five directions for how Hercule shows the work in flight. Each one draws it three ways.

24 October 2026

On a normal day you run 40 threads in five projects. The sidebar lists them and the Office counts them. Neither tells you what each one is working on, how far along it is, or what will need you next.

This book asks what a full design team would do with that problem: who sits at the table, what each person worries about, and the words they use. Then it shows the five directions that came out of it. Each one organizes the work by a different thing, and draws it three ways: in the Office, as a list of threads, and in one view of its own.

01The problem

The sidebar does all the watching, in 272 pixels

This is your sidebar on 24 October 2026. It is better than it was: #828 folded the threads of one workspace into one row with dots. But look at what a row can say.

  1. The sidebar is the only overview

    It was built to navigate: find a thread, open it. Now it also carries 40 threads in five projects, partners that leads start, threads that assistants start, and workflows that run at night. A 272px column has room for a title, a status word and a machine. Nothing else.

  2. The Office shows how many, not what

    It is the one place that shows every session at once, and you use it to show people, not to work. It does not show what each agent works on, how far it is from its goal, or which ones belong together. Every thread of a project sits in the same room, at the same kind of desk.

  3. Two ideas were left on the shelf

    The Crew Bureau 2 book drew the Office with a List | Floor switch: the beans are one way to look at a lot of work, and a list is another. Round one of the sidebar book drew Tracks, whose day sheet laid every thread on one clock.

    List | FloorCrew Bureau 2's Office, two ways The day sheetTracks, every thread on one clock

The question

How might Hercule give Rogier a true overview of all the work in flight, in three modes that look at the same work three ways: the Office, a list of threads, and one new view, so he always knows what is happening, what it means, and what will happen next?

  1. Perceptionwhat is happening
  2. Comprehensionwhat it means
  3. Projectionwhat happens next

The three levels of situational awareness. Today the sidebar gives you the first, and the Office gives you a headcount. Nothing gives you the other two.

Rogier's rules for this round

  • Up to five directions, each radically different in how it organizes the threads.
  • Three modes each: the Office (3D, with a new concept), Threads (a list, organized however the direction likes), and one view of its own.
  • Hi-fi, in Crew Bureau. New atoms are welcome when they serve the UX.
  • Intuitive to anyone who uses Claude Code, Codex or T3 Code, and still makes them think "huh, why didn't anyone think of that?"
  • Moonshots are welcome. A direction may need data Hercule does not hold yet, if it says plainly what.
  • Reviewed on the phone, over the tailnet.

02The team at the table

Five people, five different worries

Give this question to a full design team and each person pulls on a different thread. Nobody is right alone. The brief is what is left when all five have had their say.

03The lexicon

The words that framed the work

Each role reaches for its own words. These twenty did the most to shape the five directions. Each comes with a plain meaning, and an example from Hercule.

Product owner3 Lead designer3 UX expert9 Senior designer3 Junior designer2

Product owner

Is it worth building?

Jobs to be done

People "hire" a product to get a job done. A job story says when the job comes up, what you want, and why.

In Hercule"When I come back after being away, I want to know what changed, in the order it matters." That is the Catch up job, and every direction is judged on it.

Walking skeleton

The thinnest version that works from end to end. Build it first, put flesh on it later.

In HerculeExpedition's skeleton is one route line on today's row, read from the agent's own todo list, before a single mountain is drawn.

Kill criteria

A test, agreed before you build, that tells you when to stop.

In HerculeIf a mode is only ever opened to show visitors, it is a screensaver, and it is cut.

Lead designer

Is it one idea?

The axis

The one thing a direction organizes the work by. Five directions need five axes that do not overlap.

In HerculeExpedition organizes by how far from done, Control Tower by when it will need you, Atlas by where in the code, Gazette by what changed, The Firm by who works for whom.

Office Map

A Hercule word: the plan an Office is built from, with its rooms and the rule by which it grows. One Office, many possible maps.

In HerculeToday's map is the Bureau. Four directions draw new ones: the Range, the Aerodrome, the City and the Dollhouse.

The huh test

Would someone who uses Claude Code or Codex every day stop and look twice?

In HerculeControl Tower passes it with an agent tool that predicts its own interruptions, and lets you schedule them.

UX expert

Does the eye get it?

Situational awareness

Three levels: perception (what is there), comprehension (what it means) and projection (what happens next).

In Hercule"Rotate staging secrets: Working" is perception. "9 of 14 secrets rotated" is comprehension. "Will need you in about 10 minutes" is projection.

Coordinated views

Several views of one model that share the selection, the filters and the counts. Brushing and linking: pick something in one view, and it lights up in all of them.

In HerculeSelect "Refactor cart totals" in Threads, switch to the Office, and the camera flies to its bean.

Overview first, zoom and filter, details on demand

Shneiderman's mantra. Show everything at a glance, let people narrow it down, and open the details only when asked.

In HerculeThe whole Office, then the payments-api room, then the dossier card of "Payout report for September".

Semantic zoom

Zooming changes what is shown, not only how big it is.

In HerculeFar out, Atlas's City shows each folder as one block. Zoom in, and the block opens into buildings, one per file, with lit windows for today's edits.

Pre-attentive attributes

What the eye picks up before you read: hue, size, motion, position. They make a screen glanceable.

In HerculeA raised marigold hand is seen before any title is read. That is why marigold is spent only on what waits on you.

Change blindness

You miss a change because nothing marked it.

In HerculeYou come back from lunch and "Payout report" has finished, but its row looks the same as before you left.

Calm technology

Information that stays at the edge of your attention, and moves to the center only when it matters. Also called peripheral awareness.

In HerculeSix threads working is quiet background. Only "Migrate ops dashboards" asking "Keep the old Grafana folder?" turns marigold.

Spatial memory

You remember where things are, and look there again without reading.

In Herculepayments-api always sits in the same room of the Office, so your eye goes straight to it. That is also why rows never move under the pointer.

Interruption cost

An interruption costs more than the seconds it takes: you lose your focus, and need time to get it back.

In HerculeControl Tower's landing slots hold every Request that is not urgent until 10:30, so three questions cost you one break, not three.

Senior designer

Does it hold up?

Object constancy

A thing stays recognisably the same thing when the view changes, so your eye can follow it.

In HerculeThe row "Fix 3-D Secure checkout" turns into its peach bean in the Office, then into its card. Same face, same hue.

Level of detail, and label collision

Level of detail: draw less when things are far away or many. Label collision: name tags that overlap until none can be read.

In Hercule35 beans in one room, 35 tags in a pile. From afar the room shows one tag, "webshop · 12 working". Up close, each bean gets its own.

Establishing shot

A film word: the wide opening shot that shows where everything is, before the camera moves in.

In HerculeThe Office opens on the whole Range, then the camera moves in on the rope team that waits on you.

Junior designer

Who solved this already?

Teardown

Taking apart how a game, a tool or the real world already solved a problem like yours.

In HerculeThe Sims shows a thought bubble over each character. Gazette puts one over each working bean: "reading payment.ts".

Minimap

From strategy games: a small map of the whole world in a corner, with a dot per unit.

In HerculeAtlas puts a map of your code at the top of the sidebar, with a dot per agent. Tap an area and the list shows only that area.

04How the team works it

Go wide, then narrow down. Twice.

The team works in a double diamond. First it opens the problem up, then narrows it down to one question. Then it opens up the answers, and narrows them down to five directions.

  1. 1

    Discover

    What the data holds. What the Office and the sidebar do today. A teardown of games and tools. Your own sidebar on a heavy day.

  2. 2

    Define

    The job stories, the question, the "how might we" list, the principles, and the three-mode contract.

  3. 3

    Develop

    Crazy 8s for everyone, then the idea wall. Cluster by axis, pick five axes that do not overlap, and give each one a direction with its own Office Map and its own view.

  4. 4

    Deliver

    Five hi-fi live windows and five story pages, all on one shared world, compared against the job stories.

Seven jobs the overview is hired for

The product owner's job stories. Every critique checks against them, in this order.

  1. AttendWhen something needs me, I want to see it and answer it in one move, wherever it is.
  2. OverseeWhen I glance at the overview, I want to know what is moving, what is stuck and what is done, without reading every title.
  3. GaugeWhen I look at a piece of work, I want to know how far it is from done.
  4. Catch upWhen I come back after being away, I want to know what changed, in the order it matters.
  5. AnticipateWhen I plan my next hour, I want to know what will need me and when.
  6. FindWhen I want to get back to something, I want to find it by what it is.
  7. ShowWhen I show someone Hercule, I want them to understand what my agents are doing.

How might we...

The question, broken into pieces small enough to sketch.

  • ...make the Office legible: what each bean works on, and how far along it is?
  • ...let the overview see the future, not only the present?
  • ...tell Rogier what changed while he was away, in the order it matters?
  • ...keep a lone thread exactly as light as it is today?
  • ...make three modes feel like one place, not three features?
  • ...let the overview carry 40 threads today and 200 next year?

05The three-mode contract

One overview, three modes, one set of rules

The five directions disagree about how to organize the work. They agree on how the overview behaves. Every direction builds on this contract.

  • One overview, three modes. A place in the window, opened with ⌘⇧O or the sidebar's Office button. Its top bar holds a three-way switch: Office · Threads · the direction's own view. Also reachable by keyboard.
  • The sidebar is Threads, folded. Same list, same idea, at 272px. Each direction says what the sidebar does while the overview is open.
  • Coordinated views. Selecting a thread in one mode selects it in all three. Switching keeps the selection. Counts agree everywhere.
  • Waiting on you always rises, in every mode, and one move answers it.
  • A lone thread costs nothing in the list: no extra chrome, height or word.
  • Nothing moves under the pointer. Changes wait until the pointer leaves, or arrive as a pill: "3 new".
  • Cheap while idle. The Office draws only while visible and caps at 30 fps for ambient life. Nothing changes per streamed token. Each direction says what it costs.
  • Honest data. Codex reports no usage, so say "not reported", never invent a number. A prediction is shown as a prediction.
  • Crew Bureau. Color is who. State is a pose and a mark. Places get tints. Marigold means you. The beans are the real Office's beans.

Eight principles

  1. One idea, three lenses.A direction's three modes organize the work by the same thing.
  2. Comprehension, not just perception.Every mode answers "what does it mean". At least one answers "what happens next".
  3. What needs you always rises.
  4. The lone thread costs nothing.
  5. Nothing moves under the pointer.
  6. The Office tells the truth.Every visual in 3D maps to a fact. Strip the scenery and a chart remains.
  7. Calm by default.Peripheral until something needs you. Marigold is spent only on you.
  8. Cheap while idle.

Swipe the table sideways

The lead's read

No direction wins every row, and that is the point of drawing five: each one is strongest on its own axis. If Rogier asks which to build:

  • Expedition is the strongest daily tool. It adds the level nothing has today, how far along each piece of work is, to every surface: the sidebar row, the Board, the Range. Its main data ask is cheap: read the plan item Hercule already records into a number at each turn's end.
  • The Firm is the most direct answer to the Office complaint, and the cheapest first slice of all five: who started whom is already stored in sessions.created_by. The Dollhouse gives every team its own room.
  • Gazette has the best habit loop. A "since you looked" dot and a headline per turn would improve today's sidebar on their own, in any direction.
  • Control Tower and Atlas are the moonshots. Landing slots (scheduling when agents may interrupt you) and the merge forecast (a dry-run merge across every agent's branch) are ideas no other tool has. Each is worth a research ticket before a design ticket.

Atoms that travel

Small pieces any direction could adopt, whichever one is picked:

The "since you looked" dot
Gazette. Bold title and an ink dot when a thread has news you have not seen.
The stuck cloud
Expedition. "No progress in 34m", drawn on the row.
Landing slots
Control Tower. Hold what is not urgent until a time you choose.
Crossings
Atlas. Two threads in one file, shown on both rows before it becomes a merge conflict.
Stand-up

The first slice of each

  1. Expedition: the latest plan item read into {done, total}, drawn as the route on line 2.
  2. Control Tower: "busy since" on a Session, and the Holding and On stand bays. Predictions come later.
  3. Atlas: the file a thread is in now on line 2, and an Edit Overlap badge on both rows.
  4. Gazette: a last-seen mark per thread and a headline per turn on line 2.
  5. The Firm: startedBy on the contract, and partners nested under their lead.
